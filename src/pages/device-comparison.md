CrossPoint now supports a wide range of e-readers, and each one comes with a slightly different combination of screen, buttons, frontlight, touch, performance, and price. There isn’t one device that suits everyone, so we put together a practical overview of all the options — how each device feels in day-to-day use, and what its main strengths and weaknesses are.

## Xteink devices

A quick note: Xteink sells two versions of each device: a Chinese version and a global version. The Chinese version is locked against third-party firmware installation. You can still install CrossPoint, but the process is a bit more complicated and riskier. Flashing incompatible firmware can also permanently brick the device, while on the global version you can always recover it over USB.

Devices purchased directly from Xteink’s official website come with the global version. Devices from AliExpress or Taobao usually come with the Chinese version.

This is the device that started the whole "mini-reader" hype. It has been my daily driver for the past ten months, and it’s stable, simple, elegant, and feels good in the hand. Text looks crisp and the screen has very good contrast.

As for downsides, the lack of a frontlight bothers me the most. To read in bed at night, I need an external light source, like the Ulanzi M10. People also complain about the lack of a touchscreen, but personally I haven’t found it to be much of an issue. Certain actions, like renaming a file, are indeed awkward with navigation buttons only, but for my normal flow — browsing books and reading — it’s more than enough. If you spend more time using highlights, bookmarks, lookups, and customization, it will likely bother you more.

It uses the weaker ESP32-C3 processor, so some CrossPoint features don’t work here, such as USB file transfer and TTF font support, but for a reading-only device, it’s perfectly adequate.

Unfortunately, Xteink has discontinued the X4, so it is no longer available from their official store. You may still be able to find one on AliExpress or eBay.

The X4’s little brother. It adds a gyroscope, which lets you turn pages by shaking the device, and an internal clock that lets CrossPoint display the time in the status bar.

Besides that, it is smaller and lighter, and the screen has a slightly higher resolution (259 PPI compared with ~219 PPI on the X4/X4 Pro/X4 Classic). It also runs on the ESP32-C3, and it is just a tiny bit slower because of the higher screen resolution. It’s not something you’re likely to notice in normal use.

Xteink made a controversial decision here by replacing the USB-C port with pogo pins. Many people complain about this change, as it means carrying a second cable instead of just using the same USB-C cable as everything else, with the added risk of losing or damaging it. Personally, for a device that only needs charging once or twice a month, it doesn’t bother me at all. The pogo-pin connector isn’t something proprietary that Xteink invented, and cheap replacement cables are easy to find on Amazon or AliExpress.

Personally, I didn’t like the button placement.

The Sticky is technically meant to be a magnetic sticky-note-style display for your refrigerator, but it works surprisingly well as an e-reader.

When I first got my unit about two and a half months ago, it felt a little cheap in the hand, less premium than the Xteink devices. After using it for quite a while, though, I can say that it is a solid and durable device.

It also runs on the ESP32-S3 and includes a touchscreen, but no frontlight at all. If you don't do much reading in the dark, the Sticky is a great choice: excellent and very affordable at $50, sometimes dropping to $40 during promotions on Seeed’s website.

The Sticky is probably the most maker-friendly and hackable device on this list. It was designed from the start as a relatively open development platform, with good documentation, USB-C, and hardware that allows for custom development. That matters a lot to developers, though it is less relevant to average users.

As for the downsides (**important note:** my unit comes from an early production batch, so some of these issues may have been fixed in later revisions.) As I mentioned, the device feels a little cheap. The buttons are slightly loose in their slots and rattle when the device is shaken.

For some reason, Seeed chose a glossy screen rather than a matte one. Reflections from external lighting can be very annoying on an e-reader. My simple solution was to buy a half-dollar X3 screen protector from AliExpress. It isn't a perfect fit, but it fully covers the screen itself.

The SD card slot is also about a millimeter too deep. That doesn’t sound like much, but if I’ve just trimmed my fingernails, inserting or removing the card becomes nearly impossible without using some kind of tool.

The Sticky's only significant drawback is that, because of the way its USB hardware is wired internally, it does not support USB file transfer. Other ESP32-S3 features, such as TTF font support, still work normally.

Also note that the Sticky comes with no accessories at all — no SD card, no screen protector, and no USB-C cable.

EEGO first got people’s attention by promising a device that would improve significantly on the X4 while still costing less.

The A4 is made of plastic, but it feels surprisingly high-quality. It comes with a huge 2,000 mAh battery, a touchscreen, and the ESP32-S3. There are two versions: one with a frontlight and one without. The frontlit version also includes warm lighting.
